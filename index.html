Design engineering research · working prototypes

Two Worlds,
One Bridge

Two people describe the same place in ways that cannot both be true. An AI in the middle has to build something anyway. These four simulations ask what that machine does to the people talking through it — and each one is a working instrument, not a mockup.

Harvard GSD, Master in Design Engineering. The premise is Michael Reddy’s.

What the runs found

Measured, not asserted. Every figure below comes out of a run you can reproduce by opening the piece it belongs to.

A refusal outlasts a request
One thing feared outweighs several things asked for, so the crossing that gets built is shaped more by what nobody wants than by what anybody needs. And only somebody asking can bring a ground into being: refusing a thing you fear never puts a place into the world.
The artifact barely notices the conversation
Across 360 runs the machine reached the same structure every time, while what the two of them understood of each other swung from 4 to 8 sentences misread. Phrasing is free to change how badly two people understand each other without moving one matchstick.
Deadlock and mutual courtesy look identical
Both holding firm and both giving way come out at the same place. A point survives only when exactly one of them stops arguing about it. Two people deferring to each other lose precisely what two people refusing to budge lose.
The inverse of a refusal is not a want
Of the needs one party took up on the other’s behalf, 10 of 10 did nothing for the other’s actual goal. “Not cheap and done twice” is a wish for something that lasts; the only opposite available is “a great deal of it”. The generosity is real and it arrives as bulk.
A word list loses the speaker’s stance
Reading by keyword, the machine takes 77% of sentences as meant and inverts a further 12% — a refusal of climbing filed as a request for something level. It catches what is being talked about and drops whether it is wanted or feared.
A language model loses it less, not never
With real models on all three parts, the reader took 11 of 14 sentences as meant where the word list would have agreed with it on 2. So the loss is a property of reading shallowly, not of putting a machine in the middle. The constraints are a design choice, not a law.
Nobody ever says where they are standing
In every recorded run with real models, fourteen turns of describing what they needed ended over ground nobody had described. It is the one thing the machine says, unprompted, that it does not know.
Agreeing costs what you disagree about
Made to speak with one voice, the two of them drop whatever they clash over before the machine hears any of it — and it builds from what is left. Apart, each gets what they came for. Together, a compromise that serves neither.

The instruments

Four working pieces. Everything runs in the browser; nothing is sent anywhere.

01 · the simulation

Two Worlds, One Bridge

One role sees a river between two cities. The other sees a mountain between two villages. The same gap, described incompatibly — and one structure that has to stand in both. Includes the idea-space view, which shows every word said and the few the world ever picked up.

open → 02 · the experiment

I Want, I Do Not Want

Both people are simulated. One may only say “I want…”, the other only “I do not want…”, and a machine reads their sentences — not their intentions — and builds from what it takes. Five cases, four temperament pairings, 1,440 runs before the page opens, and recorded sessions where all three parts are played by real models.

open → 03 · the plate

Five Fairnesses

The same brief, solved five times under five different definitions of fairness — utilitarian, Rawlsian, Nash, precautionary, and whoever-pays. Each rule builds a different bridge. One of them builds nothing at all.

open → 04 · the instrument

Toolmakers Protocol

A session runner for studying this with real people: printable private briefs, an operator console, separate debriefs, and a comparison of what each participant left the room believing had been built.
