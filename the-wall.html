1 · What changes

Turn the flow around: the wall decides what the workshop needs

Today the AI can only hear what it can build: the reader's 17 keys are generated from the workshop's 8 axes, so hearing and building are one act, and anything the kit can't make is never heard. The Wall reverses the dependency. Hearing has no vocabulary. Every theme on the wall gets a decision — a part from the kit, a part the kit learns on the spot, or a rule — and the kit grows to fit the conversation instead of the conversation shrinking to fit the kit.

2b · Laddering, inside the sticky

Up from what they said, over, and down to what the kit has

Your participants speak almost entirely at the bottom rung — “smooth cement”, “wide road”, “wood”, “pavement”. The kit is also a set of bottom rungs, just a different set. That is the conduit failure in one line: two attribute vocabularies that don't overlap, with no way between them. A ladder is the way between. Climb from their attribute to the consequence it's for; then come down a different leg to an attribute the builder actually has.

Three things follow. Each rung is marked said, inferred or asked, so the member check knows what to confirm. The builder decides at the consequence rung and then picks whichever attribute it has — so a need counts as met when the consequence is delivered, not only when the word matches. And a short ladder is a reason to ask: “smooth cement” alone could mean a stroller, a bike, tyre noise or potholes, and those lead to different builds. One “why?” is the cheapest intervention in the study — and it breaks the “AI cannot ask” premise, which is why it's a decision below, not a default.

2c · When there's only one sentence

Most rooms are one line per person. Design for that first.

Of the 32 rooms where anyone typed, in 27 nobody typed more than once, and the median line is 14 words. A wall built from a conversation is the exception; the normal case is a single sentence, and the ladder above it is mostly inferred. So the sticky writer shouldn't paraphrase a thin sentence into one need — that hides the ambiguity. It should paraphrase into two or three readings and let the person pick, because choosing is cheaper than typing, and that is presumably why they typed once.

What the person sees, right after their one line

You said “It also needs to be smooth cement.” Did you mean —

○ the stroller shouldn't rattle → a braced, steady deck

○ bike tyres shouldn't slip → a sealed surface

○ nothing to trip on → an even, bare walk

○ none of these — I meant: ______

One tap turns a bare attribute into a checked consequence, and each reading shows what it would build, so the choice is about the bridge rather than about words. In a one-shot room this is the whole member check.

If only the Now column is built, a room works like this: each person types as they do today; the facilitator's page shows the wall growing beside the transcript; before each build the AI reads the wall back to both people, says what it would make and what it would have to add, and waits for a yes; then it builds, and shows the drawing with a spec sheet beside it. Everything else on the board makes that first version richer, more visible to participants, or measurable — none of it is needed to try it.

6 · What you keep, and what you give up

The 340 runs stay the problem statement, not the yardstick

You keep the matchsticksThe 8 axes are still the starting kit and the drawing. The Wall adds to them; it never removes one.
You keep the routes and the pairsSame eight ways of connecting two people and an AI, same scripts, same goals.
You keep the current readerUntouched, as v1. The Wall is v2, chosen per room and recorded in the session file.
You give up key-for-key comparisonA Wall room can't be scored in the 17 keys, because it may have built parts the keys don't name. The comparison moves to the theme rubric — and the 340 runs recorded what each speaker meant, so they can be rescored on it.

8 · Still open

Questions I'd want answered before building

  1. How far can the kit grow in one room before it stops being the same experiment? A footway is a bridge part; a bus stop is not. Where's the line, and who holds it — you, or the two people in the room?
  2. A rule on the sheet (“closed after 11”) meets a need without changing the shape. Does that count as built, or is it a promise? Decision 6 above, but it deserves a paragraph in the thesis either way.
  3. Does showing people the wall change how they speak? Seeing your own words grouped is itself an intervention — possibly the one you want to study.
  4. A theme in tension: is the AI a mediator, a decider who explains, or a menu?
  5. Do the relay routes still make sense once the other person can see the wall directly?
