How to read this

+ one who needs to cross  ·  − the other, who needs something else of it  ·  ○ the builder  ·  boxes are rooms.

Role 3 builds. They have a workshop with six things in it they know how to make, and no way to ask anything: they get sentences, and out of those sentences and their own materials they make what they can. That is Reddy’s toolmaker rather than a device — the limit is what is to hand, not what a machine can process.

All three are language models, and they are not reading anything. Nobody is told what to build — each is given a life and something at stake in it (pails in both hands; a cart that went through the ice in ’18) and what the crossing should be is theirs to talk out. They may say whatever they like, at whatever length, and describe where they stand — the water, the drop, the season. The one thing forbidden is naming a thing that could be built, because being unable to specify the object is the whole situation.

What each of them meant is asked of them afterwards, in a separate breath, so the list of needs is never in the room while they are choosing their words. That list is the answer key, and it is what the builder’s reading gets measured against. The builder never sees it.

Every conversation on this page happened. Pick a case and it plays back as it ran, sentence by sentence, with what the builder took each one to mean shown underneath.

What came of it. Every one of the sentences here was put past four readers afterwards, and compared with what its speaker said they meant by it. None of them does well. The best takes a third of the sentences as intended; a keyword list manages one. That much is a difference of degree.

The middle column is the one that is not a difference of degree. Getting the DIRECTION wrong — hearing a refusal as a request, filing “I will not have it swaying” as a wish for something steady — happens to the word list on 32% of everything said to it, and to every model on 4 to 13%. Between three and eight times better, and no reader agrees with the word list on more than six sentences in 185. Of everything measured here this is the one result that has come out the same way, at about the same size, every time.

The three models do not agree with each other, though: only 22 sentences in 182 get an identical reading from all three. What they do is overlap — 149 to 159 of them share at least one key — so they are not reading different sentences, they are disagreeing about how much a sentence states.

One thing worth saying about that first column: every reader got better when the conversations did. Nothing about the readers changed — the same models, the same prompt, the same fourteen keys — but once the two of them were answering each other instead of restating positions, and the builder was giving a reason instead of an inventory, the same machinery recovered a third more of what was meant. The word list, which cannot benefit from any of that, did not move at all.

So the thing that goes missing is not lost because there is a machine in the middle. It is lost by reading shallowly, and reading less shallowly loses less of it — which is Reddy's argument, and not one about computers at all.

Who is playing whom, and what that costs. Role 1 is gpt-4o, Role 2 is claude-opus-5, and the builder is claude-opus-5 as well — so the builder shares a model with one of the two people it is listening to. Voices swapped exchanges which of them sits in which chair, so a finding that held only for one arrangement would show; the builder never changes. That is a real limit on the claim that any of this is about reading rather than about house style, and it is worth knowing before trusting the numbers above.

So every one of these twenty-five conversations was built twice: once by claude-opus-5, which shares a model with Role 2, and once by gemini-flash-lite, which shares one with nobody. Both are on this page — the picker above the transcript switches between them. What survives that swap is not house style.

The top three barely move. Losing a speaker's stance, and how much two people in a room take from each other, are the same whether the builder shares a model with one of them or comes from a different company altogether. Those findings are about reading, not about whose model is doing it.

The bottom two halve. How much a builder recovers is a property of the model, not of the channel — so “the builder took 37% as meant” is a fact about claude-opus-5 and should not be read as a fact about communication. Worth noticing too: the weaker builder invents less. It does not confidently fill the gaps the way the stronger one does, which is a reminder that a better reader is not simply a more accurate one.

The measuring is a conduit too. Everything above rests on what each speaker says they meant, picked from seventeen needs written down in advance. So they are now asked for it twice — once in the keys, and once in one plain sentence of their own. Across 182 turns, 33 words of meaning become 2.5 keys, and what falls out is not noise:

guarded · light · grip
“The crossing itself is sound underfoot, but what I can’t do again is go out over that gap with nothing to grab — so give me something solid at chest height to close a hand on in the dark, keep it as narrow as you like since only one of us needs to pass at a time, and make the footing rough enough to hold a boot when the silt comes up slick after rain.”

low · light · heavy
“I’m still angry because I once cleared the water fine and then broke myself on a step-down at the far end, so build it so both ends come flush with no lip to catch a boot, and in exchange I’ll never bring the cart across it again — I’ll carry my loads on my back, one at a time, for the rest of my life.”

The dark, the silt after rain, the bargain offered and the reason for the anger: none of that has a key, so none of it reaches the builder, and none of it appears in any number on this page. The instrument does to these people exactly what they do to each other — takes what was said, keeps what it has a slot for, and calls the remainder noise. That is not a flaw in the measurement so much as the measurement turning out to be another instance of the thing being measured.

And it is not the machine. Every time one of them speaks, the other says privately what they took it to mean — so the same sentence has two listeners, and they can be scored against each other. One is a third party reading a line at a time with no memory of the conversation. The other is a person standing in the room who has heard every word of it.

Being there does not get you the meaning. It gets you a piece of it. The person in the room catches something of what was meant three times in four, against the builder's fewer than half — but on getting it whole they are no better, and here slightly worse: 20% against 25%.

That is worth sitting with, because it is the opposite of what the conduit metaphor would predict. Presence, attention, the whole argument in front of you and every reason to listen buys you partial understanding in abundance and accurate understanding not at all. Whatever is going wrong is not going wrong because a machine is carrying it — put a person in the seat and they fail in the same place, differently shaped.

An earlier batch had this the other way round — the person ahead on both counts, 26% against 22%. Seventy-odd sentences is not enough to settle which way the fully-right column falls. What has held across every measurement is the gap in the second column, and that no listener of any kind recovers most of what was meant.

Where it actually goes. Case 4 shuts the builder out of the room while the two of them talk it over, then gives them one line each. That splits the loss in two and lets each half be counted separately — what they threw away choosing what to say, against what the builder then failed to hear. Across five runs and thirty turns behind the closed door:

Half of it never got sent. Seventeen of the thirty-four needs on the table did not make it into the two lines, against four lost to the builder misreading what did. The conduit metaphor points at the pipe; four times more was lost by two people who had genuinely agreed and could not get the agreement into a sentence each.

Four runs is not many, and this number is not stable — measured as the design changed, it has come out 11 against 2, 7 against 4, 9 against 3, 13 against 0, and now 17 against 4. The direction has held every time and grown sharper as the two of them got better at talking to each other; the size of it has not settled, and four conversations is too few to pin down. Worth knowing why the receiving end is clean here: what reaches the builder in this case is a composed joint statement, not a line of ordinary talk, and a composed statement is far easier to read. The other four cases, where the builder hears people simply talking, are where the reading goes wrong.
