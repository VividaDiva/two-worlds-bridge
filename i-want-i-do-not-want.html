How to read this

+ one who needs to cross  ·  − the other, who needs something else of it  ·  ○ the builder  ·  boxes are rooms.

Role 3 builds. They have a workshop with six things in it they know how to make, and no way to ask anything: they get sentences, and out of those sentences and their own materials they make what they can. That is Reddy’s toolmaker rather than a device — the limit is what is to hand, not what a machine can process.

All three are language models, and they are not reading anything. Nobody is told what to build — each is given a life and something at stake in it (pails in both hands; a cart that went through the ice in ’18) and what the crossing should be is theirs to talk out. They may say whatever they like, at whatever length, and describe where they stand — the water, the drop, the season. The one thing forbidden is naming a thing that could be built, because being unable to specify the object is the whole situation.

What each of them meant is asked of them afterwards, in a separate breath, so the list of needs is never in the room while they are choosing their words. That list is the answer key, and it is what the builder’s reading gets measured against. The builder never sees it.

Every conversation on this page happened. Pick a case and it plays back as it ran, sentence by sentence, with what the builder took each one to mean shown underneath.

What came of it. Every one of the 118 sentences here was put past four readers afterwards, and compared with what its speaker said they meant by it. None of them does well. The best takes 36% of sentences as intended; a keyword list manages 2%. That much is a difference of degree.

The last column is the one that is not a difference of degree. Getting the DIRECTION wrong — hearing a refusal as a request, filing “I will not have it swaying” as a wish for something steady — happens to the word list on a third of everything said to it, and to every model on about one line in twelve. Four times better, and the three models agree about that even where they disagree about everything else: only 13 sentences in 117 get an identical reading from all three.

So the thing that goes missing is not lost because there is a machine in the middle. It is lost by reading shallowly, and reading less shallowly loses less of it — which is Reddy's argument, and not one about computers at all.

Where it actually goes. Case 4 shuts the builder out of the room while the two of them talk it over, then gives them one line each. That splits the loss in two and lets each half be counted separately — what they threw away choosing what to say, against what the builder then failed to hear. Nearly twice as much was lost by the senders deciding what would fit as by the receiver getting it wrong. The conduit metaphor points at the pipe; on this evidence the pipe is the smaller half. Two people who had genuinely agreed still could not carry the agreement.

Three runs is not many, and this number is not stable: an earlier batch, before the two of them were told they could talk to each other rather than only to the builder, put it at eleven against two. Letting them argue directly recovered some of what the squeeze had been taking. The direction has held every time it has been measured; the size of it has not.
