How to read this

+ one who needs to cross  ·  − the other, who needs something else of it  ·  ○ the builder  ·  boxes are rooms.

Role 3 builds. They have a workshop with six things in it they know how to make, and no way to ask anything: they get sentences, and out of those sentences and their own materials they make what they can. That is Reddy’s toolmaker rather than a device — the limit is what is to hand, not what a machine can process.

All three are language models, and they are not reading anything. Nobody is told what to build — each is given a life and something at stake in it (pails in both hands; a cart that went through the ice in ’18) and what the crossing should be is theirs to talk out. They may say whatever they like, at whatever length, and describe where they stand — the water, the drop, the season. The one thing forbidden is naming a thing that could be built, because being unable to specify the object is the whole situation.

What each of them meant is asked of them afterwards, in a separate breath, so the list of needs is never in the room while they are choosing their words. That list is the answer key, and it is what the builder’s reading gets measured against. The builder never sees it.

Every conversation on this page happened. Pick a case and it plays back as it ran, sentence by sentence, with what the builder took each one to mean shown underneath.

What came of it. Every one of the sentences here was put past four readers afterwards, and compared with what its speaker said they meant by it. None of them does well. The best takes a third of the sentences as intended; a keyword list manages one. That much is a difference of degree.

The middle column is the one that is not a difference of degree. Getting the DIRECTION wrong — hearing a refusal as a request, filing “I will not have it swaying” as a wish for something steady — happens to the word list on 42% of everything said to it, and to every model on 6 to 14%. Three to seven times better, and no reader agrees with the word list on more than four sentences in 147.

The three models do not agree with each other, though: only 16 sentences in 146 get an identical reading from all three. What they do is overlap — 109 to 128 of them share at least one key — so they are not reading different sentences, they are disagreeing about how much a sentence states.

So the thing that goes missing is not lost because there is a machine in the middle. It is lost by reading shallowly, and reading less shallowly loses less of it — which is Reddy's argument, and not one about computers at all.

Where it actually goes. Case 4 shuts the builder out of the room while the two of them talk it over, then gives them one line each. That splits the loss in two and lets each half be counted separately — what they threw away choosing what to say, against what the builder then failed to hear. Every need that was sent arrived. Not one of the twelve was misread — the builder even picked up a couple nobody had put in the line. And thirteen of the twenty-five never got sent at all. The conduit metaphor points at the pipe. Here the pipe was perfect and half the meaning still did not make it, because two people who had genuinely agreed could not get the agreement into a sentence each.

Four runs is not many, and this number is not stable — measured as the design changed, it has come out 11 against 2, 7 against 4, 9 against 3, and now 13 against 0. The direction has held every time and grown sharper as the two of them got better at talking to each other; the size of it has not settled, and four conversations is too few to pin down. Worth knowing why the receiving end is clean here: what reaches the builder in this case is a composed joint statement, not a line of ordinary talk, and a composed statement is far easier to read. The other four cases, where the builder hears people simply talking, are where the reading goes wrong.
